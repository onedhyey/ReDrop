    The problem

    Water is finite. Our habits act like it isn't.

    We turn on a tap and water appears, so it feels endless. It isn't. Population growth, water-hungry lifestyles and careless everyday use are draining the clean, safe water we depend on.

    Much of the waste hides in ordinary routines: a long shower, a tap left running while brushing, a half-empty washing load. Each one looks small. Repeated every day, by everyone on a campus, they add up to thousands of litres.

    The real trouble is that nobody can see a litre. You can't measure your shower, so you can't tell what to cut. ReDrop gives you that number.

      The ReDrop challenge

      Four weeks. One number. Go lower.

      1. Log your day

        Answer a short Google Form: flushes, shower length, the tap while brushing, laundry. It takes about two minutes. Use any name you like.

      2. We turn it into litres

        Our sheet converts every answer using standard flow rates, like 10 litres a shower-minute and 5 litres a flush.

      3. The board ranks you

        Everyone is sorted by estimated litres per day. The lowest number sits at the top.

      4. Monday, start again

        The board resets every Monday. Beat your own number, then beat your friends'.

      The research behind it

      Why a leaderboard?

      Earlier studies found that feedback, games and comparing yourself with others can cut water use. Seeing a number, and seeing your neighbour's, changes behaviour.

      Water is also stubborn. In one dormitory competition, electricity use fell 32% while water use fell only 3%. Water habits are harder to shift, which is exactly why they need attention.

      Most of those projects relied on smart meters and sensors. Our question is whether a game still works without any hardware, using only a free form, a spreadsheet and this website.

      How we're testing it

      • A four-week pilot with about 30 to 50 volunteer students.
      • Week one is a baseline with no leaderboard. Weeks two to four show the board.
      • We compare each person's average daily estimate before and after the board appears, and track how consistently people log.
      • Short questionnaires at the start and end measure awareness and what people think of ReDrop.

      What the numbers can't tell us

      • Everything is self-reported, so it depends on honest answers.
      • Ranges become midpoints: "5 to 10 minutes" counts as 7.5 minutes. These are estimates, not meter readings.
      • A small volunteer group isn't the whole campus. Any change we see is a signal, not proof.
      • Participants use aliases, and nothing that identifies you appears on the board.
